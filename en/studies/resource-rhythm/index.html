Game study / 2025.11

Clash Royale × Teamfight TacticsAuto-Battler Design: How Core Resources Shape Play

On this page
I. Comparing elixir and gold II. How the mechanics shape the experience III. How auto-battlers have eased the load on players

I chose elixir in Clash Royale and gold in Teamfight Tactics because both refill as the game progresses, yet they ask very different questions. In one, I am thinking, “How do I defend this push right now?” In the other, “If I spend this round, will my composition come together a few rounds from now?” I start with how resources turn into fighting power, then look at where an auto-battler can ease the work it asks of a player.

I. Comparing elixir and gold

What they have in common

Both resources replenish in predictable ways and limit what a player can do at a given moment. Having the card I want in hand does not mean I can afford to play it.

Throughout the match I have to choose between spending now and holding something back. Spending everything lets me do one more thing immediately, but it leaves less in reserve for the next change on the board.

Where they differ

Elixir regenerates continuously at a rate set by the game

Elixir has a cap. Waiting at full elixir gains me nothing. The cap limits how big a single push can be and gives sitting still for too long a cost.

Elixir takes a short path to the battlefield. Play a troop and it appears. Cast a spell and its effect lands. I can see almost immediately what the spend bought me.

The game sets a regeneration rate for each phase, and the rate changes between phases. I need to estimate how much elixir will come back between spending it now and the next exchange.

That leaves very little time to read the opponent’s card and decide how to answer. Attacking, defending, and catching someone short of elixir feels much like looking for an opening in a fighting game.

Gold arrives by round, and the player can change the return

Gold can be saved across rounds in much larger amounts, without an elixir-style storage cap. I can turn it into board strength now or hold it for interest and change how quickly my funds grow.

Several choices sit between gold and fighting power. Champions have different prices. Upgrading a pair depends on finding another copy in the shop. Fielding another champion may require spending gold to level up. Spend the same amount on buying champions, rerolling, or leveling, and the board comes together differently and at a different time.

II. How the mechanics shape the experience

1. Elixir creates real-time tactical exchanges

The pace is fast. The opponent already has a unit on the field. I have to decide what will answer it, when to play, and which combination gets the most from my elixir. Even the right cards can arrive too late for the situation I chose them for.

The core is response and counterplay. My cost calculation has an immediate test. Can I defend with less elixir than my opponent spent, then use what is left to counterattack?

2. Gold creates long-term economic decisions

Saving for interest, rerolling the shop, and leveling have different risks and strengthen my board at different times. Saving another round may buy extra rolls later. If the current board cannot hold, I pay for that choice in health first.

The core is prediction and planning. I weigh my Augments, the champions the shop offers, and the other boards, then decide whether to play for a win streak or take losses to build my economy. At rounds such as 3-2 or 4-2, I decide whether to spend heavily and turn those savings into a stronger team.

III. How auto-battlers have eased the load on players

During the sets I studied, Teamfight Tactics introduced a new set roughly every four months. Each one meant learning new champions, compositions, and mechanics. For someone who knew the previous set, the hardest part was often the beginning. I knew I had a decision to make, but I did not yet know what it would do in this set. Four changes made it easier to handle items, manage health, understand champions, and get things done during a round.

1. Predictable Magnetic Remover drops

In the version I studied, PvE rounds at each stage dropped Magnetic Removers on a fixed schedule. Previously, giving an item to a temporary carry raised another question. If I wanted to keep that champion but move the item later, would a remover drop in time? Relying on a random drop made building an item early a risk.

Once removers became predictable, I could use an item to solve the problem in front of me and change its owner later. What to build and when to build it still mattered. I could put useful components to work without holding them for a lucky drop.

2. Changing health loss after a defeat

Losing early puts less pressure on health, and surviving enemy units affect how much damage a loss deals. Even when I cannot win, taking out one or two more units matters. Economy-focused players have more room to adjust in stages four and five. A slow composition has a chance to reach its power spike, leaving room to balance teams that come online at different times.

3. Reworking how champions gain mana

When mana generation follows a champion’s role, “How does this champion get to cast?” gives me a way into positioning and item choices. On meeting a new champion, I can start from the role instead of having to memorize a separate rule for every unit.

4. Organizing items on a bench

When components were scattered on the ground, I had to reroll, buy, reposition, and combine items in the same planning phase. Even when I knew what I wanted to build, locating the components took time. In a rush I could combine the wrong ones.

With components in fixed slots, I can see where they are, combine an item on the bench, and give the finished item to a champion. The time saved on finding and dragging components goes back into deciding who should hold the item.

Changes I would try next

1. A team planner that starts from my current board

Use the champions already on the board to show late-game compositions I could transition into, with suggestions for units and positioning.

Design goal and reasoning

Design goal: Give players more ways to transition and ease the pressure of rebuilding a team while rerolling late in the game.

Design reasoning: Early on, I use what the shop offers and field the pairs I can make. Later, my ideal carry might never appear. Pivoting may need cheap units I passed up earlier. By the time the expensive carry arrives, its low-cost partners are hard to find, and the board gets stuck halfway. A planner could show several destinations from the board I already have, while there is still time to keep the units I will need.

2. Return to the board

Record the opponents’ actions and the random events in a match. After the game, let the player return to an earlier board state and change their own play.

Design goal and reasoning

Design goal: Let players test their own post-match judgments firsthand.

Design reasoning: After losing a round, I might think a last-second positioning swap would have won it. Next game the opponent, shop, and board will all be different. Returning to the same fight would let me change just the move I am curious about and see what difference it actually made.

3. A practice board

Give players a board with free access to champions, adjustable star levels and equipment, and an enemy composition they can set up before starting a fight.

Design goal and reasoning

Design goal: Give players a place to experiment at no cost. They could try new-set combinations repeatedly and set up opponents to test team strength and positioning counters.

Design reasoning: Testing one combination in a live match means waiting for units, saving gold, building items, and risking health. The start of a new set is when I most want to ask, “What happens if I put these two champions together?” It is also when those conditions are hardest to assemble. A practice board would put the conditions in my hands, so I could learn the interaction and take my own judgment into the next match.
