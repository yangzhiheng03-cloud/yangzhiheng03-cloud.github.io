One anchor, multiple possibilities

Our four-person team made this 2D platform-adventure puzzle prototype for the 2026 CiGA Game Jam theme, “Anchor.” After the 48-hour jam, we spent two weeks refining the mechanics and levels. Players throw an anchor to create footholds and break walls, then combine it with a chain to move and dive.

I was responsible for mechanics design, level design, and level building, working with the programmer on implementation adjustments. The other two members mainly contributed review and feedback.

Design goal: let observation, experimentation, combination, and revisiting reveal more uses for the same tool.

Reaching the end completes the game. Optional collectibles reward closer observation and more advanced experimentation.

From discovery to deliberate use

Turn an anchor into a foothold

The anchor becomes a platform when it lands or embeds in a wall. A height gap immediately after acquiring it encourages players to create their own foothold. Later gaps ask them to repeat and internalize the technique.

Make connection change movement

Once the chain is acquired, the anchor can pull the player. Players learn to attach it, wait for it to settle, and reel themselves onto a platform, then practice movement and detachment. Chain length can only change once the anchor stops.

Turn an old obstacle into an entrance

The first encounter with water keeps players at the surface through buoyancy. With an anchor and chain, the sinking anchor pulls them underwater. A familiar obstacle becomes a new route on the return journey.

Make experimentation less punishing

Air control allows landing adjustments. A 0.1-second coyote-time window and a 0.1-second jump buffer support more forgiving platforming. Spikes return players to an activated flag checkpoint so they can retry the current challenge.

A hub and a return loop

Room 04 joins several routes. Players first cross room 01 at the water surface. After acquiring the chain, they dive through rooms 11, 12, and 13, return to the upper part of 01, and reach 14. The new ability changes how they understand an already familiar space.

Show the destination before it is reachable

The destination in room 10 is visible behind a wall on the first visit, but reached later through rooms 14 and 15. Room 07 revisits the wall-breaking rule: a throw breaks a thin wall on the first pass, while a falling anchor breaks a thick wall on the return to the hub.

The final Shadow Play sequence expands “create a foothold” into “change the walkable structure.” Players inspect the gaps, adjust the anchor position, and cross the fixed shadows.

From paper to playable space

I sketched platform placement, water, and room connections before building them in Unity and checking traversal. These pairs show how platforms, lower exits, and return routes became playable spaces.

Segmented chain: an implementation change becomes a lesson

To make connection and reeling better communicate the anchor’s weight, the programmer and I discussed replacing a rope approach with a chain that has segment collisions. I then added a reel-up sequence so players could learn the relationship between chain length and their position.

Keep an advanced interaction on an optional route

The programmer found that jumping from the anchor’s one-way platform in a narrow passage could move the player through a wall. After evaluating it together, we kept the behavior for an optional collectible route. The main path does not require it because of its execution difficulty and learning cost.

What I would test earlier next time

A mechanic that makes sense on paper may not feel natural in play. Pulling, collisions, and reeling need to be tested in playable spaces before their level-design implications are settled.

If I were to plan the process again, I would build a small test level earlier to check collisions, pulling, and chain-length changes before expanding the full map. That would help catch issues before they create rework.
