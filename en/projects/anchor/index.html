Anchor Sailor

2026.07
Overview
A sailor explores an interconnected platforming level with an anchor and chain. The same anchor can make a foothold, hold a connection or open a route through water.
Goals
Let a new use for a familiar tool change how an old room reads. Returning to the first pool should become a puzzle about the anchor the player already has.
Engine
Unity
Genre
2D puzzle platformer
Project duration
16 days (2 for the jam, 14 for iteration)
Team & production
Four-person CiGA Game Jam team. I designed the mechanics and levels, built the rooms and contributed 2D art.

Inspiration & Research

2026 CIGA / 48h Game Jam / Two weeks of iteration

I explored weight, fixing, throwing and connection, then built obstacles and routes around them. Experimentation, combinations and revisits reveal new uses for the same tool.

I made this project with three teammates for the 2026 CIGA Game Jam theme, "Anchor". I led mechanic design, level design and level building. We developed it in 48 hours, then spent two weeks refining the gameplay and levels. Animal Well's Bubble Wand inspired me: players can stand on bubbles, create more in midair to climb, and open new routes. I wanted one anchor to offer the same range of possibilities: create footholds, break walls, and use a chain to dive or fly upward.

Teamwork & Making Process

2026 CIGA / Team of 4 / 48-hour development + two weeks of iteration

I led mechanic design, level design and level building, and discussed implementation changes with the programmer. Our other two teammates reviewed the work and provided feedback.

MAKING & ITERATION

I sketched platforms, water and room connections, then built them in the engine and checked the playable routes.

REFLECTION

Paper exploration: platform heights, drops and places where an extra foothold is needed.

03 / Looping routes

Paper exploration: upper and lower connections, return routes and sequences of obstacles.

To make pulling and reeling in feel consistent with the anchor's weight, I discussed the physics with the programmer. We replaced the rope with a segmented chain that has physical collisions. I added a climb where players fix the anchor and reel in, teaching how chain length affects their position.

The programmer found that jumping from the anchor's one-way platform in a narrow passage could push the player through a wall. We reviewed the behavior together and kept it for optional collection routes. Its execution and learning cost were too high to require it on the main route.

This collaboration taught me that mechanics need constant adjustment through play. A design on paper cannot guarantee a good experience. It needs to be tested in real gameplay, with feedback guiding the next change.

When the programmer reported a problem, I first clarified the intended player experience. We then discussed implementation and brought the decision back into the level. The segmented chain became part of the main learning path; wall clipping became an optional collection technique. These changes helped me decide what every player should learn and what to leave for deeper experimentation.

I could have built small test rooms earlier to check collisions, pulling and reeling in. This would have exposed problems before I built the full level and reduced later rework.
